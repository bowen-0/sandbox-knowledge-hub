# External reference: AI-adoption drivers in public organizations (Guedes & Oliveira, 2024)

> [!update-needed]
> **This page is built entirely from a source outside the Zurich AI Innovation Sandbox corpus** — see [[guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector]]. It exists to let a reader compare the sandbox's own findings against an independently-derived study, not to extend the sandbox's evidence base. Nothing here should be cited as a Zurich-corpus finding, folded into `phase-2-cross-cutting-principles`, or treated as `sandbox-operations` guidance — the source paper has no sandbox-programme dimension at all; it studies AI adoption inside operating public organizations directly.

## What the external source found

Guedes & Oliveira (2024) coded ten semi-structured interviews with public- and private-sector executives plus a case study of Brazil's Federal Revenue Service (RFB) customs AI system, SISAM, into ten ranked adoption drivers: efficiency, workload, implementation and operations, privacy, quality, leadership, transparency, fairness and justice, trust, and responsibility, in that order of coded frequency [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 14)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md). Efficiency dominated, but the paper stresses that transparency, technological readiness, skilled labor, fairness, and privacy all substantially shaped whether adoption succeeded [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 14)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md).

Three findings stand out as directly comparable to sandbox-corpus themes:

**1. Leadership support as a precondition, not a formality.** Eight of ten interviewees treated AI adoption as a strategic decision requiring explicit top-management backing on safety and privacy grounds, and the SISAM case interviewee went as far as saying they would hesitate to even start an AI project without it [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 16)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md). This echoes — but is a separately-sourced, independent data point from — the Zurich corpus's own [[strong-stakeholder-engagement-is-decisive]] and [[value-must-be-clearly-articulated]] lessons.

**2. Human accountability by design.** The paper's SISAM interviewee frames it sharply: a machine that cannot be accountable for an outcome cannot be allowed to make the decision [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 13)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md) — and the deployed system reports its reasoning in natural language while leaving field agents free to override or independently examine any case [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 13)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md). This is the same "human stays in charge" pattern the Zurich corpus documents in [[human-oversight-in-ai-deployment]], arrived at independently in a Brazilian tax-enforcement context rather than a Swiss administrative one.

**3. No formal business case required.** Against prior literature holding that a solid AI business case is a prerequisite (Chui et al., 2018, as cited in the source), SISAM's development was initiated and executed by senior leadership without one [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 16)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md) — a data point worth weighing against, not automatically generalizing to, the Zurich pilots' own procurement and scoping patterns (see [[partner-roles-around-a-pilot]]).

## Where it diverges from the Zurich corpus

- **No sandbox structure.** RFB built and owns SISAM directly; there is no equivalent of an innovation-sandbox intermediary, test-partner/technical-implementation-partner split, or the Zurich corpus's `partner_role` taxonomy. Comparisons should stop at the adoption-driver level, not extend to programme design.
- **No EU AI Act or Swiss-law framing.** The paper's regulatory discussion is high-level (citing the 2023 "pause giant AI experiments" open letter, OECD/EU ethics guidelines) rather than jurisdiction-specific; it does not engage with [[eu-ai-act]], [[dsg-fadp]], or any instrument in the Zurich corpus's `regulations/` set.
- **Different AI paradigm.** SISAM is described as a Bayesian-network classification/estimation system trained on ~100 million records [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 14)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md), closer in kind to the Zurich corpus's [[computer-vision]]/classical-ML pilots (e.g. [[smart-parking]], [[infrastructure-maintenance]]) than to the generative-AI-era Phase II pilots. Its lessons about model choice should not be read as generative-AI findings.
- **Broader ESG framing.** The paper folds AI adoption into an explicit environmental-social-governance (ESG) argument, including a cited carbon-footprint estimate for large-model training [(guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector p. 18)](../sources/guedes-oliveira-2024-ai-public-sector.md), which has no direct counterpart in the current Zurich corpus.

## How to use this page

Use it when a reader explicitly asks how the sandbox's findings compare to outside evidence, or asks about public-sector AI adoption drivers in general and the corpus's own [[phase-2-cross-cutting-principles]] and driver-related lessons should be supplemented with an outside data point. Do not surface it as if it were sandbox-corpus evidence, and do not merge its ten drivers into the Zurich corpus's own lesson/theme structure.
