# The economic case for the Sandbox — the two-minute version

> **For:** a minister, a finance committee, or a programme designer who needs the strategic argument for funding a sandbox, not the technical detail. This is `insight_domain: sandbox-operations` — it is about why a government would run such a programme, not how to deploy an AI system.

The headline a minister should hear first: the Sandbox is an economic competitiveness instrument that happens to produce AI governance, not a governance project that happens to help the economy. Canton Zurich's own framing in the Phase II booklet: *"In this way, the AI sandbox acted as a catalyst for a broader, strategic approach to AI policy."* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 24)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md) The programme sits inside the *Amt für Wirtschaft* (the Office of Economic Affairs), and the Government Council decision it fed into is titled "Strengthening the AI Ecosystem" ([[zh-ai-ecosystem-strategy-2026-2029]]). The economic frame is the corpus's own, not a gloss laid on top of it.

## The argument in five moves

**Uncertainty is the tax on AI investment.** Three uncertainties slow private and public AI deployment: whether a use case is legally permissible, whether it can be made to work in a real operating context, and whether the data and test environment to prove it even exist. Each is a reason an organisation defers an investment or never starts. The Sandbox's first level of impact is described as exactly this barrier reduction — real-condition support for development, testing and validation, access to test partners and public-sector data, and *"Regulatory clarification: Structured classification of legal requirements and reduction of uncertainty"* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 20)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md).

**Removing that uncertainty moves prototypes to deployment, faster and with more confidence.** The booklet states that this first impact level *«schafft direkten Mehrwert und beschleunigt die Umsetzung von KI-Innovationen»* — creates direct added value and accelerates the implementation of AI innovations (translation ours; this sentence appears only in the German edition) [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 20)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md). The evidence that this is real rather than aspirational is that pilots crossed into production: the [[building-permits]] prototype's results fed directly into the cantonal parliament's response to a postulate on AI in the permit process [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 12)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md), the [[machine-translation]] pilot was rolled out across several cantons, and [[smart-parking]] went into productive use in Frauenfeld and was expanded [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 5)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md).

**It makes the region a more attractive place to build and deploy AI.** By giving local providers structured access to real operating environments and supervisory engagement, the programme strengthens the local ecosystem as a development site. The booklet records that the approach *"evolved from a cantonal initiative in Zurich into an internationally recognised reference model, attracting significant interest beyond Switzerland"* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 4)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md) — the attractiveness argument turned outward.

**It connects the actors who otherwise don't meet at a working table.** A sandbox project convenes a test partner, a technical implementation partner, mandated experts, and a supervisory authority around one concrete use case ([[partner-roles-around-a-pilot]]). The know-how-transfer level extends this beyond the parties to the project: published reports, reusable prototypes opened to third parties (the [[building-permits]] and [[digital-eye-clinic]] platforms), regulatory roundtables, and international exchange with regulators in Germany and Liechtenstein [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 21)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md).

**The knowledge outlives the projects, and feeds evidence-based policy.** The corpus's strongest single statement of who benefits comes from the conclusion: *"Local AI providers and public institutions benefited through increased legal certainty, supervisory authorities gained practice-oriented foundations for proportionate regulation, and policymakers received evidence-based decision-making inputs."* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 23)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md) The systemic impact is concrete: sandbox findings fed the Federal Council's decision on AI regulation, which anchored sandboxes as an innovation-promotion instrument, and transferred to other sectors via *Educa*, *Swissmedic* and *MODI* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 21)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md).

The same evidence base lets a regulator separate the barriers worth removing from the safeguards worth keeping. Running inside existing law, the pilots showed where rules were a genuine constraint and where the uncertainty was the only obstacle — which is what makes proportionate regulation, rather than precaution-by-default, an evidence-based choice rather than a guess.

> [!ai-deployment] One claim here is deployment-side, not programme-side
> The "prototypes to deployable solutions" benefit is partly grounded in the corpus's deployment finding that *scaling fails on integration, not on model performance* ([[integration-matters-more-than-model-choice]]). That is advice for a team deploying a system. The minister-facing point is the programme-level one: the Sandbox de-risks the path *to* that deployment by clarifying the legal and operational unknowns up front.

## The disproportionate-return claim

The strategic close is that a small, targeted public investment can generate ecosystem-level returns out of proportion to its size — the leverage runs through reused datasets, reusable legal analysis, and convened partners, not through the headcount of the team. This is the corpus's own logic but stated at portfolio level: no single report quantifies a return per franc. The supporting mechanisms are documented — a published, annotated infrastructure-inspection dataset that becomes a public benchmark for many providers ([[data-reuse-multiplies-pilot-impact]]), prototypes opened for third-party development, and the three-level impact model that carries a single project's value into the wider ecosystem and into policy [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 21)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md). Treat "disproportionate return" as a portfolio-synthesis claim resting on these mechanisms, not as a measured figure.

## What it cannot promise

Proportionality matters most here, because this is the question most likely to slide into a pitch. The honest limits are stated in the booklet itself, in the section immediately before the conclusion.

The model is high-touch and small-scale. *"Operating a sandbox is resource-intensive. Scaling from five to, for example, fifty projects would require substantial personnel and organisational investment … nevertheless, natural limits to scalability remain."* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 22)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md) Each use case needs tailored legal, technical and domain support; the call cadence of roughly every 1.5 years means good ideas can wait. So the instrument's reach is bounded by design — its value is depth and transfer, not volume.

The benefits are ecosystem-level and partly indirect. The Sandbox demonstrates what works and de-risks deployment; it does not itself deploy at scale. The booklet is explicit that large-scale roll-out rests with the institutions — *"Broad-scale adoption, however, remains a shared responsibility across the entire AI ecosystem."* [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 22)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md) Continuation of a tested pilot is the project partner's responsibility, not the programme's [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 5)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md). The case to a minister is therefore a case for an ecosystem multiplier, not a guaranteed per-franc return on a line item.

And the evidence space is bounded by existing law. Running every project within current rules makes the findings immediately usable, but it cannot explore approaches that are not yet legally permissible — so the Sandbox surfaces where safeguards genuinely bite without being able to test past them [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 22)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md). The booklet's own outlook proposes a politically-cleared *"expanded testing scope"* under strict supervision to address exactly this limit [(Build & Share (Phase II overview report), p. 23)](../sources/00-overview-phase2-build-and-share.md).

> [!update-needed] Reflects Phase II practice (2024–2026)
> This case rests on the Phase II final report. The four-pillar 2026–2029 strategy ([[zh-ai-ecosystem-strategy-2026-2029]]) explicitly widens the frame from single pilots to a standing ecosystem, with new SME and start-up instruments alongside the Sandbox — so the economic argument a minister hears in 2027 will sit inside that wider programme, and the scaling and cadence limits above may be partly answered by the more agile intake the outlook proposes.
